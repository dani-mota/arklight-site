The electrician shortage
isn't coming. It's here.

By 2030, the U.S. will be short roughly 80,000 electricians. The math is already locked in: aging journeymen, declining apprenticeship completion, and a demand curve bent steeply upward by data-center, EV, and reindustrialization buildout. The deficit is structural — and it is binding the things America says it wants to build.

Dani Mota
Founder · Project Arklight
7 min read

There is no version of the next decade where the U.S. builds the data centers, the chip fabs, the shipyards, and the grid upgrades it has already committed to — without dramatically more electricians than it currently has any plan to produce.

Most workforce conversations treat the electrician shortage as a near-term hiring problem. A hot labor market. A pandemic distortion. Something that resolves with higher wages and a bit of patience. The data says otherwise. The shortage is structural, demographic, and accelerating — and the country has already missed the window where it could have been solved with conventional means.

1. Demand is bending faster than supply

Between 2015 and 2023, U.S. electrician demand grew at roughly 2.0% per year. Between 2024 and 2030, it is on track to grow at 4.6% per year — driven primarily by three things at once: AI data-center buildout, transmission and grid modernization, and the electrification of light vehicles and industrial heat.

Meanwhile, supply has grown at roughly 1.2% per year and is decelerating as retirements accelerate.

2. The workforce is aging out

The median U.S. electrician is 42 years old, with 22% of the workforce already over 55. Retirement-rate math says the country will lose roughly 26,000 journeymen per year through 2032, replaced by an apprenticeship pipeline that completes only ~18,000 per year. That's a steady-state deficit before demand growth.

3. The apprenticeship system is leaking

The U.S. registered roughly 78,000 new electrical apprentices in 2024. That sounds healthy until you trace completion: 38% never finish year 4, and of those who do, ~15% leave the trade within five years for adjacent work (project management, supervision, or out of the field entirely).

Run the math: 78k starts × 62% completion × 85% retention = ~41k net new journeymen, against ~67k annual replacement demand. The leak is in year 2, where wage progression flattens and the apprentice often discovers they can earn comparable money in less physically demanding work.

4. The demand isn't evenly distributed

Aggregate numbers hide the binding constraint. The shortage is concentrated in three buildout categories that don't substitute for each other: data centers, transmission, and EV/charging infrastructure. Each requires different skill loadouts and certifications.

"You can't appropriate your way out of a labor cliff. The capital is there. The contracts are there. The people aren't."

What it means

Three implications follow:

  • Bid math from 2022 doesn't work. The fully loaded marginal electrician will run ~$148/hr by 2028 in tier-1 metros. GCs and developers using 2022 unit economics are quietly underwriting losses.
  • The 4-year apprenticeship is the wrong instrument. Not because it produces bad operators — it produces excellent ones — but because the country cannot wait four years to begin closing a gap that is already binding. The pipeline needs to run in parallel, not sequentially.
  • The training-system shortage is the root cause. The talent exists. The buildings, the curricula, the partner-employer relationships, and the modern pedagogy do not. This is what Trade School 2.0 was designed to address.

None of this is a story about "the trades being uncool." Wages in the field are up 41% real over the last decade. Interest is up. Applications to electrical programs are up. The bottleneck is not demand for the work. It is the institution responsible for turning that demand into operators.

That institution has not been rebuilt in seventy years. We're building it now.
